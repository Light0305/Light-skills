# From protected facts to a publishable argument

Use for substantial drafting or an authorized structural edit. Numerical fidelity
alone does not make a paragraph useful. Select the operations relevant to the
section; these are choices, not a mandatory sentence template.

## Advance the argument

Before drafting, choose the paragraph's answer and the evidence relationship that
makes it informative: a trade-off, convergence, discrepancy, qualification,
conceptual distinction, or boundary of a theorem. Connect that relationship to
the question the reader needs answered. Do not merely restate the source facts
and append limitations.

- In an Introduction, connect the unresolved issue to the proposed approach and
  explain what the documented comparison can establish. Close on a scoped
  contribution or question. An inventory of missing evaluations rarely provides
  that ending; retain only the limitations needed to define the contribution.
- In a Discussion, distinguish an interpretation from an alternative and explain
  why their difference matters. A proposed next test is useful when its plausible
  outcomes would change the interpretation in different ways. Say what each
  outcome would support without treating a single experiment as uniquely proving
  a mechanism. For theoretical or interpretive work, the discriminating operation
  may instead be a boundary case, a proof obligation, or a source comparison.
- In qualitative or interpretive findings, use the relation among supplied themes
  or passages to develop an explicit reading. Keep it marked as interpretation;
  do not invent quotations, events, frequency, motives, or author intent.

Reverse-outline the draft by the work each sentence performs. If two sentences
only paraphrase the same relationship, combine them. Use the freed space to
clarify a supported distinction, consequence, or competing explanation. Never
pad a minimum length with repeated caveats or invent evidence to add depth.

## Write in the manuscript's voice

Keep provenance in working notes; use manuscript-native language in the prose.
“A supplied counterexample” generally becomes “a counterexample”; “the
author-supplied analysis identifies” becomes “the analysis identifies.” Do not
silently turn “no proof was supplied” into the literature-wide claim that no
proof exists: state that the extension is conjectural in this work, or flag the
missing proof in an author note when material.

Teaching-fixture labels and task metadata do not normally belong in manuscript
prose. Preserve “synthetic” when it describes an actual dataset, method, or study
design, rather than merely labeling a fictional evaluation example. Preserve
documented attribution to a participant, narrator, source author, or scholar.

## End with what remains established

After a necessary qualification, make the supported answer clear. Replace a
chain of generic “does not establish” statements with the specific inferential
boundary and its consequence where possible. Do not weaken a clear observation
merely to sound cautious; do not dismiss an uncertain or contrary result to make
the ending stronger. A strong ending need not claim novelty, impact, or utility
that the evidence does not support.
