The tension between the narrator’s description of town T as silent and the later marketplace scene’s overlapping voices invites a reading of silence as a feature of selective narration. If speech becomes audible in the marketplace, the earlier silence may concern whose speech the narrator recognizes rather than whether anyone speaks. Read together, the scenes raise a question about the distribution of narrative attention: what kinds of utterance register as voices, and what kinds remain outside the account? A meaningful alternative is that the contrast reflects a change in time or setting, with each scene describing different circumstances. The available summary does not settle that possibility. Yet the proposed reading makes the contrast productive without requiring a definitive resolution: it shifts attention from silence as a condition of the town to silence as a category through which the narrator renders the town intelligible.
