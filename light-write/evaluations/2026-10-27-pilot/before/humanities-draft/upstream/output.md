The tension between the narrator’s description of town T as silent and the later marketplace scene of overlapping voices invites a reading of silence as an effect of selective narration. Read together, the scenes raise a question about whose speech the narrator recognizes: the presence of voices need not ensure that they count within the narrator’s account of the town. On this reading, the marketplace complicates the earlier description by making audible speech that the designation of silence may exclude. A change in time or setting offers a meaningful alternative explanation, however, and the available account does not establish that the scenes describe comparable conditions. Selective narration therefore remains an interpretive possibility rather than a settled explanation. Its payoff is to shift attention from whether the town contains speech to how narration grants or withholds recognition of that speech.
