# Physical sciences and engineering

- **ID:** physical-sciences-engineering
- **Maturity:** draft
- **Scope:** Writing about measurements, physical models, materials, devices, simulations, and engineered systems within supplied conditions.
- **Out of scope:** All subdisciplinary conventions; instrument validation, safety certification, or current publisher/venue rules.
- **Provenance:** General editorial starting guide; no specialist review or performance evaluation.
- **Last reviewed:** Not reviewed

## Argument and evidence load

Distinguish theoretical derivation, numerical simulation, laboratory measurement, prototype performance, and field operation. Define conditions, scale, boundary assumptions, and uncertainty at which a contribution is supported. Do not infer that agreement in one regime establishes a model's universal validity.

## Writing decisions

- **Abstract synthesis:** Keep theoretical predictions, adjusted-model predictions,
  and physical measurements distinct. Use their relationship to explain the
  design insight. State a missing matched comparison or untested operating range
  where it changes that interpretation, rather than repeating the same unresolved
  comparison in the result sentence and the final contribution sentence.
- **Discriminating follow-up:** When asked to propose a test of a physical
  explanation, specify the factor to vary, the competing outcome to observe,
  and relevant conditions to hold comparable. Label these as proposed controls,
  never as procedures already performed. A vague request for more experiments
  does not explain how the hypothesis would be assessed.

- **Motivation:** State the physical or engineering limitation in a defined operating regime. Connect the proposed model/device to that limitation, with a meaningful comparator when supplied.
- **Methods:** Preserve geometry, material/sample preparation, boundary conditions, apparatus, calibration, discretization, units, and analysis details actually documented. Mark missing reproducibility details without inventing tolerances or procedures.
- **Results:** Keep predicted and measured values distinguishable. Report units, uncertainty definitions, and operating conditions; do not equate numerical precision with experimental accuracy.
- **Comparison:** Distinguish performance, efficiency, stability, reliability, and scalability. A prototype at one scale does not establish manufacturability, lifecycle performance, or certification. Simulation cannot be described as experimental demonstration.
- **Discussion:** Explain the range in which the evidence supports the physical interpretation; state competing explanations or untested regimes. Keep model assumptions and material trade-offs visible.
- **Language:** Maintain dimensional consistency and symbol definitions from the source. Do not convert a standard error to instrument uncertainty, or a simulated parameter sweep to measured replication, without explicit basis.

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Corrective check |
|---|---|
| Simulation result is called a measured demonstration | What was computed and what was measured? |
| More significant digits imply higher accuracy | What uncertainty/calibration information supports precision? |
| One operating point becomes universal superiority | Which regimes and comparators were tested? |
| Prototype results imply commercial readiness | Is scaling, reliability, or certification evidence supplied? |
