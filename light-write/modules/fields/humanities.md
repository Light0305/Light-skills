# Humanities and interpretive scholarship

- **ID:** humanities
- **Maturity:** draft
- **Scope:** Argumentative and interpretive scholarly writing, including textual/historical analysis, conceptual work, and source-based interpretation.
- **Out of scope:** A uniform method for all humanities disciplines, fabricated textual evidence, source verification, or venue-specific policies.
- **Provenance:** Editorial starting guide; no disciplinary-expert review or empirical output evaluation.
- **Last reviewed:** Not reviewed

## Argument and evidence load

Identify the interpretive or conceptual claim, the source base, the reasoning that connects them, and plausible alternatives. Strong scholarship may rely on close reading, archival evidence, philosophical argument, or contextual analysis rather than experimental Results. Do not frame all contributions as measured effects or universally novel discoveries.

## Writing decisions

- **Opening:** Establish the interpretive problem, debate, object, or tension relevant to the intended readership. Context must support the argument rather than serve as generic background.
- **Sources:** Distinguish primary material, secondary scholarship, translation, paraphrase, and the author's interpretation. Do not invent quotations, page numbers, editions, provenance, archival identifiers, or historical events.
- **Argument:** Make each section advance a claim using specific supplied evidence and explicit reasoning. Preserve tensions and counter-readings that bear on the interpretation; avoid reducing a nuanced position to a simplified opponent for rhetorical force.
- **Method:** Explain the documented analytic approach and corpus selection where pertinent. Do not require a laboratory-style methods section or infer representativeness from a small text corpus.
- **Interpretation:** Attribute voice carefully: what a source states, what a narrator/participant claims, and what the scholar argues are different. Do not turn an ambiguous passage into definitive author intent without support.
- **Interpretive payoff:** A live counter-reading limits exclusivity, but need
  not erase what a defensible reading makes visible. Explain which relation in
  the supplied material the reading foregrounds even while the alternative
  remains unresolved. Do not replace that contribution with a question that
  implies interpretation is worthless until one reading is decisively proved.
- **Language:** Preserve historically or conceptually significant distinctions, transliterations, quotations, and author-defined vocabulary. Translation choices that change an argument need explicit author review rather than silent modernization.
- **Conclusion:** State what the reading or argument contributes and its scope. Do not generalize from selected texts to an entire era or culture without supplied support.

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Corrective check |
|---|---|
| A quoted character's voice becomes author intent | Whose claim or voice is being represented? |
| Missing evidence is filled with plausible quotations | Is the wording and locator actually supplied or verified? |
| Empirical-paper structure is imposed | What structure advances this interpretive argument? |
| Translation erases a conceptual distinction | Does the revised term preserve the argument's meaning? |

## Interactions

Combine with social-behavioral for interpretive social inquiry and with article-type guidance for review or perspective pieces. Do not impose statistical evidence requirements on non-statistical claims, or excuse unsupported factual assertions because a paper is interpretive.
