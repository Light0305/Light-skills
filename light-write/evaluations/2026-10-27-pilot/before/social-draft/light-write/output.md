The analysis of interviews with 18 purposively sampled nurses from two wards at one hospital identified scheduling autonomy as qualified by informal obligation. Accounts of the new shift-allocation policy described greater autonomy over scheduling alongside pressure to cover shifts. These themes were not simply opposing evaluations offered in separate accounts: some participants described both, indicating that greater choice and constraints on exercising it could coexist. Formal autonomy therefore did not necessarily mean freedom from informal expectations. The tension concerned the relationship between having scheduling choices and feeling obliged to cover shifts, rather than a straightforward distinction between autonomy and its absence. Within these accounts, the policy’s significance lay in this coexistence: greater scheduling autonomy remained subject to informal pressure that limited its exercise.
